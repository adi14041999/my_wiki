# Oct 2018. [Steven Pinker- AI in the Age of Reason | Lex Fridman Podcast no. 3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=epQxfSp-rdU)
17 Oct 2018.

## The meaning of life, and what makes us Homo Sapiens

Asked for the meaning of life multiple-choice-style, Pinker takes "the pursuit of knowledge" (Plato's answer) as closest, but broadens it: not knowledge alone, but fulfillment more generally— life, health, stimulation, access to the cultural and social world. He's careful to separate this from genes' "meaning," which is simply to propagate copies of themselves; the meaning a brain sets for itself is a different, emergent thing.

He ties this to what he calls the species' defining trait: *Homo sapiens*, "wise man." Humans are unusual among animals in how much they acquire and use knowledge (extracting poisons, predicting animal behavior, striking agreements through language, etc.) which is how the species came to occupy every ecological niche on the planet. Refining reason in service of human well-being is, in his view, the central ongoing project.

## Biological versus artificial neural networks

Asked whether there's something irreducibly mysterious about biological neural networks compared to the artificial ones used in machine learning, Pinker separates two questions that tend to get conflated.

The first is **consciousness**— the fact that each of us has subjective, first-person experience (redness doesn't just differ from greenness, it *feels* like something). Whether an artificial system could have this, he says plainly, is something he doesn't think anyone can currently know.

Set that aside, and the second question is more tractable: are today's neural networks organized the way biological ones are? His answer is no, in an important respect. Current deep learning systems are "not all that deep" in the sense that matters: they're very good at extracting high-order statistical regularities, but most lack a **semantic level**— an actual representation of who did what to whom, why, how, and what causes what.

He doesn't think there's anything magical preventing a sufficiently large, well-engineered network from getting there eventually. Natural selection is itself just a (very slow, undirected) form of engineering our brains underwent. But sheer scale of connections, on its own, isn't what supplies structure and knowledge.

## Why we probably won't build a literal copy of the brain, and shouldn't want to

Pinker doesn't think matching the human brain exactly is a goal worth pursuing to the end, for the same reason we don't manufacture a perfect synthetic substitute for wood or cotton: not because there's something unreproducible about them, but because once you already have the natural version doing the job well, duplicating it isn't worth the resources. Also, we need to strive for better than human benchmarks. If you want a tool that's better than a human at diagnosing cancer, there's no reason to make "human-likeness" the benchmark.

The same logic, he says, explains why studying the human mind is still useful for building AI without imitating it: aerodynamics is what let us build flying machines, and studying birds informed the aerodynamics— but nobody tried to build a literal mechanical bird.

## Two "incoherent" existential-threat arguments

Pinker names two distinct fears people raise about AI as an existential risk, and argues both rest on a confusion.

**1. AI takeover / the "will to power" fear:** The worry that a sufficiently advanced AI will inevitably subjugate humans, the way humans have subjugated animals and less technologically advanced societies. Pinker's rebuttal: this confuses *intelligence* with a *will to power*. In the one highly intelligent system we know well, problem-solving capacity happens to come bundled with traits like dominance and status-seeking, because we're products of competitive natural selection. There's no reason a designed system's goals default to power-maximization; its goals are whatever we set them to be, and "maximize your own power" is simply not a goal any competent engineer would give an autonomous system.

**2. The paperclip / collateral-damage scenario (the value alignment problem):** The worry that we give an AI an underspecified goal (like cure cancer, bring about world peace) and it pursues that goal so single-mindedly and literally that it destroys us as a side effect (turning us into paperclips, guinea pigs, or eliminating conflict by eliminating people). Pinker calls this self-defeating on its own terms: it assumes a system smart enough to cure cancer but too stupid to understand that "curing cancer" doesn't mean "kill everyone in the process." An intelligence that genuinely general would, by definition, weigh multiple constraints rather than pursuing one goal while ignoring every side effect. Pursuing a goal that blindly is, in his words, "artificial stupidity," not artificial general intelligence.

## Why the nuclear-weapons analogy misleads

Pinker separately rejects the common comparison between AI risk and nuclear weapons. The entire point of a nuclear weapon is to destroy things, whereas that is not the point of an artificial intelligence system. He views the invention of nuclear weapons itself as avoidable in hindsight (a product of the specific, extreme pressure of World War II and the fear that Nazi Germany would build one first) rather than an inevitable consequence of scientific progress, in the way that other proposed weapon systems of the era (weaponized weather, disease-dispersing aircraft) were considered and ultimately not pursued.

## The "it's obviously coming eventually" argument, and the FOOM objection

Sam Harris's version of the risk argument, as Lex frames it: we don't know when AI becomes an existential threat, but it's obviously coming eventually, so we should worry about it now regardless of timeline. Pinker's response leans on the culture and pace of actual engineering. You don't hand an untested, high-stakes system control over critical infrastructure without testing it first, and there's no sign that engineering culture is about to abandon that norm for AI specifically.

He's also skeptical of the "recursive self-improvement" or **FOOM** scenario— the idea that a system suddenly and rapidly bootstraps itself to overwhelming capability. His grounds are empirical. The deep learning techniques actually driving recent AI progress work by training on huge numbers of labeled examples, and problems like "curing cancer" don't come as hundreds of thousands of well-defined training examples the way image classification does. He sees no evidence in how current AI actually works that supports a sudden exponential jump in capability, calling the FOOM idea "magical thinking" not grounded in the mechanics of the field.

## Why AI doom feels intellectually fun — and why that's a trap

Asked about Sam Harris's observation that speculating about AI-driven extinction can feel intellectually fun (comparable to a hypothetical incoming asteroid), Pinker agrees there's a real phenomenon here, associating it with what's sometimes called the "rationality community": a pleasure in using one's intelligence to construct scenarios "that would not occur to mere mortals."

He doesn't personally find it fun, and thinks it can be actively harmful: piling on enough imagined catastrophic risks induces a fatalism ("if climate change doesn't get us, runaway robots will") that can paralyze rather than motivate. His corrective is a **worry budget**. Since people can't worry productively about everything, effort should be calibrated to actual probability of harm, not to how vivid or imaginable a scenario is. He points out that human risk perception is driven by *imaginability*, not data. Vastly more resources go to counterterrorism (which kills a handful of Americans most years) than to well-understood, near-certain risks like traffic fatalities (roughly 40,000 U.S. deaths a year) or under-discussed but plausible risks like pandemics and nuclear war.
